# Teaching and group learning

[Collection](../README.md) · [Learn](README.md) · [Topics](../topics/README.md) · [Methods](../methods/README.md) · [Resources](../resources/README.md) · [Community](../community/README.md)

Use these options to build a course module, seminar, or reading group around a question and a small observable learning outcome. Adapt the scope and assessment to the learners and available time. A group can include learners with different backgrounds by assigning complementary perspectives rather than placing people into fixed tracks.

## Choose a format

| Format | Useful when | A practical structure |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Short course module | Learners need a common conceptual vocabulary | Question → model or measurement → guided exercise → comparison and limitations |
| Reading group | Participants want to examine assumptions and evidence in papers | Pre-reading prompt → claim map → methods and evidence → alternative explanation → open question |
| Mixed-background workshop | Participants bring different expertise | Shared scientific question → parallel biology, mathematics, computation, and measurement lenses → joint explanation |
| No-code discussion | The learning goal is conceptual or analytical rather than implementation | Figure or equation walk-through → prediction before reveal → compare outcomes → discuss what is and is not supported |

## Reusable learning outcomes

After a session or short sequence, learners should be able to:

- state a scientific question and distinguish it from a method or tool;
- explain what a model represents, what it omits, and what it predicts;
- connect a measurement to its units, sampling, and plausible limitations;
- identify a baseline or control that would test a conclusion;
- distinguish a model's predictive success from evidence for a biological mechanism; and
- record enough methods and provenance for another person to inspect the reasoning.

Choose only outcomes that fit the session. For a mathematical session, assess assumptions and derivation; for a measurement session, assess interpretation and quality control; for a computational session, assess choices and reproducibility as well as code.

## Adaptable session plans

### One-session model discussion

Choose a question from the [topic guides](../topics/README.md) and one introductory [book, course, or review](../resources/README.md). Ask learners to identify model variables, observables, and one omitted biological feature. Then ask what evidence could distinguish the model from an alternative. Assess with a one-page claim map: question, assumptions, predicted observable, comparison, and limitation.

### Reading group on a paper

Select a paper or review from [papers and reviews](../resources/papers-and-reviews.md) and pair it with its relevant [topic guide](../topics/README.md). Have participants summarize separately the claim, the method, the evidence, and the interpretation. In discussion, ask what comparison or control bears on the main claim and what a different outcome would mean. Assess through a short written critique that cites a figure or method and states one remaining uncertainty.

### Cross-background model clinic

Use one model or dataset to invite several lenses: what is biologically represented, what mathematical assumptions are made, what the code or analysis computes, and what was measured experimentally. The group creates one joint diagram or table that defines terms and records an analogy's limits. Participation can be assessed by asking each learner to state one connection and one mismatch in their own terms.

### No-code evidence and theory session

Use a published figure or a derivation from a selected [topic](../topics/README.md) or [method](../methods/README.md). Before showing the reported result, ask learners to sketch what they expect under the proposed account and under a simpler alternative. Compare the predictions with the figure or derivation, then discuss which assumptions are necessary. No installation or programming is required. Assess with a prediction table or a brief explanation of what the evidence does not establish.

## Make exercises accessible and informative

Offer a conceptual, paper-based, or coding option for the same scientific question. Define unfamiliar notation, label units, provide an accessible plain-text description of figures, and state software or data requirements before the session. If a coding environment is used, provide a small, documented starting example and an equivalent interpretation task.

Use the [prerequisite self-checks](prerequisites.md) to identify optional review material, and the [suggested projects](projects.md) to choose a bounded activity. Give feedback on the learner's reasoning, assumptions, controls, and interpretation; tool fluency is only one possible outcome.

For a graph-and-evidence session, compare the scales in [connectomics](../topics/connectomics.md) or the alternative growth rules in [development](../topics/development-and-self-organization.md). For a methods clinic, use [simulation-based inference](../methods/simulation-based-inference.md) or [causal inference and design](../methods/causal-inference-and-experimental-design.md) to ask what an estimate depends on. Keep each activity bounded to one graph, inference target, or comparison.
